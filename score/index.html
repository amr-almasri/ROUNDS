The Rounds Evidence Score

Learn more about The Rounds Evidence Score and how it is calculated

Introduction

PubMed lists more than 1.8 million records with a 2025 publication date, about 5,000 a day, by a search run on 27 October 2026. As early as 2010 that was already 75 trials and 11 systematic reviews a day.1 Orthopaedics carries its share across trauma, arthroplasty, sports medicine, spine, hand, upper extremity, foot and ankle, oncology and paediatrics. Rounds reads 44 journals, almost all of them orthopaedic, from JBJS and The Bone & Joint Journal to the subspecialty titles, alongside society, regulatory and education sources.

Orthopaedic surgeons read between cases, on call and after clinic, and like other physicians they rely on the abstract. Internists in one survey read only the abstract for 63% of the articles they looked at.3 One estimate put the time a physician trained in epidemiology would need to appraise a single month of the literature relevant to primary care at 627 hours.2 The difficulty is rarely a shortage of good research. It is knowing where to start.

The Rounds Evidence Score is a way to triage that volume. Every abstract is read against the same fixed rules, and the papers that report the most decision-relevant evidence rise to the top, so a reader with twenty minutes spends them on the papers most likely to bear on their practice.

A low score is not a verdict that a paper is poor. Biomechanical and cadaveric work, basic science, early case series and technique papers are what later orthopaedic trials are built on: a fixation construct is tested on the bench before it is tested in patients. Many score modestly because they answer a different question from the one a clinician brings to clinic, not because they were done badly.

The score is built for anyone keeping up with the literature who needs a place to start — surgeons, fellows, residents, medical students and researchers alike. An academic reader can use it the same way, as a first pass rather than a replacement for their own reading.

Evidence Based Model

The score takes its structure from established reporting and appraisal methods.

Score aspectWhat it is built on
CriteriaThe published standards for what an abstract must report: CONSORT for Abstracts for trials4 and PRISMA for Abstracts for reviews.5
ModulesEach module follows its design's own reporting guideline, among them STROBE,15 STARD,16 TRIPOD,17 PRISMA,10 AMSTAR 2,11 GRADE14 and CARE.18
Checks and SafeguardsTrials with inadequate or unclear allocation concealment overestimated treatment effects by 30–41%, and trials that were not double-blind by 17%.6
Item by itemSummary quality scores can rank the same trials in opposite directions depending on the scale used, so methods should be judged item by item.7
ConclusionsAbstract conclusions often overstate trials with non-significant results: 58% in one sample.8 Conclusions are flagged, never scored.
OverallA study done by McMaster, McMaster PLUS, screened more than 110 journals against appraisal criteria before clinicians rated what passed, and 90% of physicians offered the alerts used them.9

The Score

The Rounds Evidence Score is absolute: there is no partial credit. Every item is earned in full or not at all.

  1. 1
    Identify the design

    The publication type and the design the abstract states decide which module analyses the paper. An editorial, letter or comment is not research and scores 0.

  2. 2
    Check the integrity record

    A retraction or an expression of concern stops the paper here.

  3. 3
    Find each aspect in the abstract

    For each aspect the model answers one question: is this stated, and in what words?

  4. 4
    Check the quote

    If those exact words are not in the abstract, the aspect counts as not reported.

  5. 5
    Score the five domains

    Each aspect carries a fixed weight for this kind of paper, and is given in full or not at all.

  6. 6
    Apply any correction

    A paper carrying an erratum loses points and is flagged.

  7. 7
    Apply the label

    From the number and a final checklist of the methods this design depends on.

A retracted paper is never scored

A retraction or an expression of concern on the record scores 0, moves the paper to Background, and the reader is warned on the card itself. Rounds never presents a withdrawn paper as ordinary reading.

What reported means

The score checks that a method was reported, not that it was done well. A trial whose abstract says “patients were randomized” earns the randomization points; how the sequence was generated is not read, because an abstract does not say. Judging method quality from an abstract would not be reliable, and nobody could check it. A quoted phrase can be checked in seconds.

The five domains

Every paper is analysed against five domains.

Design
Can this design answer this kind of question?
Decision information
Does the abstract state the population, the comparator, the outcome, the main estimate and its uncertainty?
Safeguards
Are the methods this design depends on reported? Safeguards carry the most weight, because they are the part a reader cannot get from the title.
Size and precision
How much was studied, and how precisely is the result reported?
Transparency
Who funded it, is the record clean, and is the data available?

The five domains are scored separately and added together. The total is the Rounds Evidence Score, from 0 to 100. The weight each domain carries is fixed in advance and applied identically to every paper of the same kind.

Modules

Research comes in many shapes, and a question that matters for one design means nothing for another. Rounds therefore analyses each paper with the module that fits its question, and every module has its own ceiling: the highest score that kind of paper can reach when its abstract reports everything the module asks for.

RES Limitation

  • The RES only reads the abstract, which can be constrained by the character counts journals impose. A thin abstract scores low however good the study behind it is.
  • It checks that a method was reported, not that it was adequate.
  • It has not been validated yet.
  • It is not RoB 2, AMSTAR 2 or GRADE, and it is not a verdict on quality.

EXAMPLE 3 · WORTH A LOOK

Definitions and reporting of ‘failure’ after rotator cuff repair

Cecere M, et al.  ·  Shoulder & Elbow, 2026  ·  doi 10.1177/17585732261501972  ·  full abstract.

Decision informationDesignSafeguardsSize
Background: Failure is widely reported in randomised controlled trials assessing rotator cuff repair (RCR), yet the term lacks a consensus definition and reported rates vary widely. This systematic review characterised how RCR failure is defined and reported in Level I and II studies and proposes a reporting standard. Methods: MEDLINE, Embase and Cochrane were searched for English-language Level of Evidence I and II studies published between January 2003 and April 2025 reporting on failure following RCR. Data on definitions, parameters and etiologies of failure were extracted and summarised through descriptive statistics. Results: Twenty-two studies met the inclusion criteria. Ten (45.5%) provided a formal definition of failure; the remainder described specific etiologies. Imaging-based assessment of tendon integrity was the most common parameter among formal definitions (7 of 10), and re-tear was the most common etiology among studies without one (9 of 12). Reported failure rates tracked closely with the parameter used, exceeding 50% under imaging-based definitions and reaching 0% under a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM)-based definition. Discussion: RCR trials lack a uniform definition of failure. We propose a multi-domain reporting standard incorporating structural, patient-reported and clinical outcomes, with thresholds to be established through expert consensus.

The RES — Frequently asked questions

The Rounds Evidence Score

What is the score for?

Triage. More than 1.8 million records reached PubMed in 2025, and no clinician can read more than a fraction of them. The score orders each day's papers so the ones that report the most decision-relevant evidence come first.

What does the number mean?

How much of what a reader needs to judge a result is stated in the abstract, out of 100.

Who is it for?

Anyone keeping up with the literature who needs a place to start: surgeons, fellows, residents, medical students and researchers. An academic reader can use it the same way, as a first pass rather than a replacement for their own reading.

Is a low score a criticism of the study?

No. It often means a short abstract, or a paper answering a question that is not a clinical one. Basic science, case series and technique papers are what later trials are built on, and many score modestly for that reason.

Is it a percentile?

No. The score belongs to the paper alone. Other papers do not move it, and any number of papers can score 100.

Is it based on evidence?

The structure is. What it checks for comes from the published standards for abstracts, CONSORT and PRISMA for Abstracts,4,5 and each module follows its own design's reporting guideline. Allocation concealment and blinding weigh more because trials without them have been shown to overstate treatment effects.6 The point values themselves were set by judgement. The sources are listed at the end.

Is it validated?

Not yet. It is deterministic and auditable, and we never describe it as validated or clinically verified. A validation study is planned: blinded clinicians and methodologists ranking papers against one another, to see whether the score's order matches theirs.

Is it GRADE, RoB 2 or AMSTAR 2?

No. Those need the full methods.11,13,14 The score checks the abstract for what those tools ask a paper to report. It replaces none of them.

How the Score is calculated

Does AI decide the score?

No. A model reads the abstract and quotes what it states. Fixed rules in ordinary code assign every point and every label.

What stops the model making something up?

Every fact comes back with a quote, and the quote is checked against the abstract automatically. If those exact words are not there, the fact counts as not reported and earns nothing. A claim the model invented cannot survive that check.

Why do different papers get different questions?

Randomization matters for a treatment trial and means nothing for a diagnostic study. Each paper is read by the module for its kind of question.

Do the journal or citations count?

No. Neither do impact factor, novelty, statistical significance, or whether the result was positive.

Rationale

The method is in the paper. Why was it not counted?

Our model only examines the abstract. A method described in the full text but not in the abstract earns nothing.

We reported p-values. Why did we lose the uncertainty points?

A p-value says an effect was found, not how large it might be. Uncertainty needs a confidence interval or a stated error. This is the most common reason a strong paper scores lower than expected.

My trial scored 90 and is only Worth a look.

Key reading needs 85 and the final checklist. For a trial that is: a randomized design, the main estimate, its uncertainty, randomization, and loss to follow-up. One of those is missing from the abstract.

Our meta-analysis pooled 400,000 patients and scored below a trial.

Size credit for a review comes from the number of studies it included rather than the patients those studies enrolled, because the patient count is a property of the field rather than of the review. A review is scored on how it was conducted: its protocol, its search, its appraisal of the included studies, its synthesis method, and a pooled estimate reported with its interval.

Can a guideline be Key reading?

Yes, when its abstract states where the evidence came from, how its certainty was graded, how the panel reached its recommendations, and its funding and conflicts.

Why are conflicts required for guidelines and nothing else?

Because a guideline is the one kind of paper a clinician may act on directly, without weighing the study behind it, so who funded the panel is part of reading it. For every other design, declaring funding earns points rather than being required.

Can a case report be Key reading?

No. Within our model, case reports, narrative reviews, laboratory studies and general research have lower ceilings and no final checklist. The model is a triage that sets reading order; it is not a judgement on the paper.

Does industry funding lower the score?

No. It raises a flag the reader sees. Declaring industry funding earns exactly the same credit as declaring any other funding.

What is “design uncertain”?

The publication type and the abstract disagree about the design. Both are kept, and the paper is not promoted until the disagreement is resolved — by a person reading the two and recording which is right.

What happens to a retracted paper?

It scores 0, moves to Background, and the reader is warned on the card. An erratum costs 5 points in total and is flagged.

Corrections and audit

Can I see how my paper was scored?

We keep the full record of every score — which module read the paper, which aspects were found, and the exact words that earned each one. We do not publish those ledgers, but if you think a paper of yours was read wrongly, write to hello@getrounds.ca and we will check it against the abstract.

What if my paper was put in the wrong module?

Write to hello@getrounds.ca. We check the paper against the stored quotes and rescore it if it was misread.

Can I appeal a score?

Unfortunately we are not able to offer appeals: the arithmetic is fixed and applied identically to every paper, so there is nothing in it to appeal. What we do review is whether we read the abstract correctly — a missed aspect or the wrong module — and we correct those.

Why does the app not show the breakdown?

Readers get the number, the label, and one sentence on what the abstract covers and leaves out. A points table pulls attention to the arithmetic instead of the paper. The method is published; the point-by-point calculation is proprietary.

Will the weights change?

Very possibly. We are always looking to improve the model, and validation is likely to tell us where it is wrong. Any change is a new version applied by rescoring, and older scores keep the version that produced them.

Does reading or saving a paper change its score?

No. Nothing a reader does affects it.
